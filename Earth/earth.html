Namesake

Every other planet in the solar system carries the name of a Greek or Roman god. Earth is the exception — its name traces back roughly a thousand years to an old Germanic word that simply means "the ground."

Potential for life

Earth sits at a temperature and chemical sweet spot that has let life flourish for billions of years. What sets it apart most is liquid water — most of the planet is covered in it, and conditions here have kept it liquid long enough for life to take hold in the oceans an estimated 3.8 billion years ago. Some of the very conditions that make Earth so livable are now shifting because of ongoing climate change.

That tilt is why the northern and southern hemispheres take turns leaning toward the Sun over the year — more direct sunlight brings summer to whichever half is tilted in, while the other cools into winter. Around the spring and fall equinoxes, both hemispheres get roughly equal sunlight.

Moons

Earth is the only planet in the solar system with exactly one moon. Beyond lighting up our night sky, the Moon plays a quiet but important role in keeping Earth livable — its gravity steadies our planet's wobble, which has kept the climate more stable over long stretches of time.

Most moons are captured rocks, but ours likely formed differently: billions of years ago, a large object is thought to have slammed into a young Earth, flinging out debris that eventually clumped together into the Moon. At about 1,738 km in radius, it's the fifth-largest moon in the solar system, orbiting roughly 384,400 km away — far enough that about 30 Earth-sized planets could line up in the gap between us.

Earth also occasionally picks up temporary companions: small asteroids that get briefly caught by its gravity for months or years before drifting back into their own orbit around the Sun.

Rings

Earth has no rings.

Formation

Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago, as gravity gathered swirling gas and dust into the third planet from the Sun. Like the other rocky planets, it ended up with a central core, a rocky mantle, and a solid crust.

Structure

Earth is built from four main layers. At the center sits a solid inner core of iron and nickel, about 1,221 km in radius, where temperatures reach as high as 5,400°C. Around it, a liquid outer core of the same metals forms a shell roughly 2,300 km thick.

Above that is the mantle — the thickest layer, at about 2,900 km, made of hot, slow-flowing rock with a consistency often compared to caramel. Finally, the crust forms the outer shell, running about 30 km deep under continents but thinning to just 5 km beneath the ocean floor.

Surface

Like Mars and Venus, Earth has volcanoes, mountains, and valleys — but its outer shell is also broken into huge moving plates. The North American plate, for instance, drifts west over the Pacific basin at roughly the pace your fingernails grow. Earthquakes happen where these plates grind past each other, collide into mountain ranges, or pull apart.

Oceans cover about 71% of the planet, averaging 3.6 km deep and holding 97% of Earth's water. Most of Earth's volcanoes are hidden beneath that water — Hawaii's Mauna Kea is actually taller than Mount Everest from base to peak, just mostly submerged — and the planet's longest mountain range runs along the Atlantic and Arctic seafloors, stretching four times the combined length of the Andes, Rockies, and Himalayas.

Atmosphere

Earth's atmosphere near the surface is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, and 1% other gases like argon and carbon dioxide — a balance that happens to be exactly what we need to breathe. Beyond supporting life directly, it shapes our climate and weather, blocks much of the Sun's harmful radiation, and burns up most incoming meteoroids before they ever reach the ground.

Magnetosphere

Earth's fast spin and molten iron-nickel core generate a magnetic field, which the solar wind bends into a teardrop shape trailing away from the Sun. When charged particles from that wind get funneled toward the poles, they collide with air molecules and light up the sky as the aurorae.

This same field is what makes a compass needle point north — though its polarity isn't permanent. On average, roughly every 300,000 years, Earth's magnetic field flips direction entirely, though the timing is far from regular and there's no evidence it has ever harmed life here. A reversal isn't expected for at least another thousand years, but when one does occur, compasses would point in scattered directions for a few centuries before eventually settling to point south instead of north.
