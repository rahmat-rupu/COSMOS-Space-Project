Mars

The fourth planet from the Sun — cold, dusty, and reddish from rusted iron in its soil

Introduction

No other planet has been studied up close quite like Mars — it's the only one we've driven rovers across. Those missions have turned up strong evidence that billions of years ago, Mars was a warmer, wetter world with a much thicker atmosphere than it has today.

Namesake

The Romans named the planet after their god of war, struck by its blood-red color. Other ancient cultures made the same connection — the Egyptians, for instance, called it "the red one." That reddish tint comes from iron minerals in the surface dust that have oxidized, essentially rusting the planet's soil, which is why Mars is still nicknamed the Red Planet today.

Potential for life

Nobody expects to find anything alive on Mars right now. The search is really aimed at the past — evidence of life that might have existed when the planet was warmer and had liquid water on its surface.

Mars has seasons much like Earth's, but they run longer and unevenly, since its orbit is more oval-shaped and the planet takes almost twice as long to circle the Sun. Its northern spring stretches nearly 194 sols, while northern autumn wraps up in about 142.

Moons

Mars has two small, lumpy moons, Phobos and Deimos, likely asteroids the planet captured long ago — they're too small for their own gravity to pull them into a round shape. Both are named after the horses that, in Greek myth, pulled the war god Ares' chariot.

Phobos, the bigger and closer of the two, is scarred with craters and long grooves, and it's slowly spiraling inward — in roughly 50 million years it will either crash into Mars or break apart. Deimos sits farther out, is about half Phobos' size, and looks smoother because loose dust has settled into most of its craters.

Rings

Mars has no rings today. That could change eventually — when Phobos eventually breaks apart, the debris may settle into a faint dusty ring around the planet.

Formation

Mars took shape about 4.5 billion years ago as gravity gathered gas and dust into the fourth planet from the Sun. Like the other rocky planets, it ended up with a central core, a rocky mantle, and a solid outer crust — just at roughly half Earth's scale.

Structure

At its center, Mars has a dense core of iron, nickel, and sulfur spanning roughly 1,500 to 2,100 km in radius. Around that sits a rocky mantle about 1,240 to 1,880 km thick, topped by a crust of iron, magnesium, aluminum, calcium, and potassium — only 10 to 50 km deep.

Surface

Up close, Mars shows browns, golds, and tans, but the rust-colored iron dust that coats everything and drifts into the atmosphere is what gives it that reddish glow from a distance. Despite being about half Earth's diameter, Mars has almost as much dry land surface area, shaped over eons by volcanoes, impact craters, shifting crust, and dust storms.

Its Valles Marineris canyon system is the largest in the solar system — long enough to stretch across the continental United States, about 3,870 km long, up to 600 km wide, and as deep as 9.3 km in places, dwarfing Earth's Grand Canyon many times over. Mars is also home to Olympus Mons, the solar system's tallest volcano, rising more than 40 km from base to summit — over four times the height of Mount Everest — with a base roughly the size of Arizona.

Water on Mars

Ancient river channels, deltas, and lakebeds point to a Mars that once had abundant liquid water, possibly including major floods around 3.5 billion years ago. Water hasn't disappeared entirely — today it survives as ice just beneath the polar surface and as occasional salty water that seeps down certain slopes, since the thin atmosphere can't keep liquid water stable on the surface for long.

Atmosphere

Mars' atmosphere is thin and dominated by carbon dioxide, with some nitrogen and argon. Dust suspended in the air gives the sky a hazy, reddish cast rather than Earth's blue, and the thin air offers little shielding from incoming meteorites or comets.

Temperatures swing wildly, from about 20°C on a warm day down to roughly -153°C at the coldest — because the thin atmosphere can't hold onto the Sun's heat. Standing at the equator at noon, your feet might feel like spring while your head feels like winter. Occasional planet-wide dust storms can take months to fully settle.

Magnetosphere

Mars has no global magnetic field today. Patches of strongly magnetized crust in the southern hemisphere, though, are leftover evidence of a planet-wide magnetic field that existed around 4 billion years ago.
