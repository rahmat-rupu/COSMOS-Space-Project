Saturn

The sixth planet from the Sun, known for its sweeping ring system

Namesake

Saturn is the most distant of the planets that ancient stargazers could see without a telescope, so it has been tracked in the sky since antiquity. Its name comes from the Roman god of agriculture and wealth — who, in the old myths, was also said to be the father of Jupiter.

Potential for life

Saturn itself is a poor candidate for life: its temperatures, crushing pressures, and chemical makeup are far too extreme for any known organism. Its moons tell a different story. Two of them, Titan and Enceladus, hold internal liquid oceans beneath their surfaces — conditions that make them some of the more promising places to search for life in the solar system.

Because its tilt is similar to Earth's, Saturn goes through seasons of its own over the course of its long orbit.

Moons

Saturn has more confirmed moons than any other planet in the solar system — over 270 and counting, as new ones continue to be spotted and officially named. Among the standouts: Titan, wrapped in a thick, hazy atmosphere of its own, and Enceladus, whose icy shell hides a liquid ocean underneath.

Rings

Saturn's rings are made of countless fragments of ice and rock, ranging from grains of dust to chunks the size of a house — likely the leftovers of comets, asteroids, or moons that wandered too close and were torn apart by Saturn's gravity. Spread out, the ring system reaches roughly 282,000 km from the planet, yet in most places it's only about 10 meters thick.

The rings are labeled alphabetically in the order they were discovered rather than by position. Moving outward from the planet, the sequence runs D, C, B, then a gap called the Cassini Division, followed by A, F, G, and finally a faint outer E ring.

Formation

Saturn came together about 4.5 billion years ago, when gravity gathered swirling gas and dust into the gas giant we see today. Like Jupiter, it's built almost entirely from hydrogen and helium — the same elements that make up most of the Sun.

Structure

Beneath its clouds, Saturn hides a metallic core of iron, nickel, and rock, wrapped in layers of liquid metallic hydrogen and then liquid hydrogen. Curiously, Saturn is the only planet in the solar system less dense than water — if there were an ocean big enough to hold it, Saturn would float.

Surface and atmosphere

As a gas giant, Saturn has no solid ground to stand on — what looks like a surface is really just deeper, denser layers of gas and liquid. Its visible bands of yellow, brown, and gray come from clouds and jet streams racing around the planet, some blowing at over four times the speed of the strongest hurricane winds on Earth.

Saturn's north pole hosts a striking six-sided jet stream, first spotted by the Voyager probes decades ago. Research published in 2026 revealed a newly identified counterpart at the south pole: a slower, ten-sided wave pattern, built from data going back to 2023.

Magnetosphere

Saturn's magnetic field is weaker than Jupiter's but still roughly 578 times stronger than Earth's, and it's large enough to envelop the rings and most of the planet's moons. Unusually, much of Saturn's aurora activity seems to be driven by particles shed from its moons and by the planet's own fast rotation, rather than by the solar wind the way Earth's auroras are.
