Overview

Venus is the second planet from the Sun and the closest planet to Earth. After the Sun and Moon, it's the brightest thing in our sky — and unusually, it spins backward compared with most of the other planets.

Close in size and structure to Earth, Venus is sometimes nicknamed our "evil twin." A thick atmosphere traps heat so effectively that it's turned into a runaway greenhouse — surface temperatures reach about 467°C, hot enough to melt lead, under air pressure roughly 93 times what we feel at sea level on Earth. Beneath its permanent cloud cover lies a landscape of volcanoes and warped mountain ranges.

Namesake

The ancient Romans could pick out seven bright objects moving across the sky — the Sun, the Moon, and the five brightest planets — and named each after an important god. Venus was named for the Roman goddess of love and beauty, known to the Greeks as Aphrodite. It's the only planet named after a female deity, and most of its surface features are named for women as well.

Potential for life

About 50 km above Venus' surface, conditions look surprisingly different: temperatures range from roughly 30 to 70°C, and the air pressure resembles what we experience on Earth's surface — a range that could, in theory, support hardy microbial life.

At the very tops of Venus' clouds, where winds reach up to 360 km/h, scientists have spotted persistent dark streaks that absorb ultraviolet light and refuse to break apart even in hurricane-force winds — and nobody has fully explained why. Leading theories point to fine dust, ice crystals, or an iron-chloride compound, though a small number of astrobiologists have floated a more speculative idea: that the streaks could involve microbial life adapted to Venus' clouds, possibly shielded by sulfur-based chemical coatings that also happen to absorb damaging UV light. Soviet Venera probes did detect micron-sized particles in the lower atmosphere — about the size of a bacterium — though this is far from proof of anything living there. Between the missing ocean, the volcanic history, and these open questions, Venus remains a compelling target for future missions.

Because Venus orbits closer to the Sun than Earth does, it never strays far from the Sun in our sky — which is why ancient skywatchers once mistook it for two separate objects, a "morning star" and an "evening star." At its closest, Venus comes within about 38 million km of Earth; at its farthest, the two drift about 261 million km apart. Watched through a telescope over months, Venus even cycles through phases like the Moon — though a full cycle takes 584 days rather than one month, and it was exactly this observation, first made by Galileo, that helped confirm the Sun-centered model of the solar system.

Venus rotates so slowly, and backward, that a sunrise-to-sunset stretch takes about 117 Earth days — and the Sun would rise in the west and set in the east. With almost no axial tilt, Venus never gets the seasonal variation Earth experiences.

Moons

Venus is one of only two planets in the solar system without a real moon. It does, however, have a quasi-satellite — an asteroid that loosely tags along with the planet without being truly captured in orbit around it — officially named Zoozve. Discovered in 2002, it's estimated to be somewhere between 200 and 500 meters across, and researchers believe it may have been tracking Venus for at least 7,000 years, likely nudged into its current path by Earth's gravity.

Zoozve's name has an unusual origin: it started life as the provisional designation "2002 VE," which an illustrator creating a children's poster misread from his own handwriting as "Zoozve." The name stuck after a radio show tracked down the story, and the International Astronomical Union officially adopted it in 2024.

Rings

Venus has no rings.

Formation

Venus and Earth started out remarkably alike — similar size, similar internal structure, and both likely had oceans in their early history — yet they ended up on almost opposite paths, one a lush living world, the other a scorching inferno. Both formed roughly 4.6 billion years ago from the same swirling disk of gas and dust that built the rest of the solar system, and understanding what pushed them apart could tell us a lot about how habitable planets form in general.

Structure

Sliced in half, Venus and Earth would look strikingly similar: an iron core, a thick hot-rock mantle, and a thin rocky crust on top. Heat and pressure beneath that crust drive volcanic activity on both worlds. Earth's crust is broken into plates that drift slowly over time — plate tectonics — and there's evidence Venus may have had something similar early on, with subduction (one plate sliding beneath another) possibly still active there today. NASA's Magellan spacecraft mapped Venus by radar through the early 1990s and found a surface reshaped by intense, relatively recent volcanic activity.

Surface

Venus holds the record for hottest planet, with surface temperatures around 467°C and pressure equivalent to being more than 900 meters underwater on Earth. Soviet Venera probes that landed between 1961 and 1984 survived only briefly — the longest for two hours — but managed to send back images of a dim, rocky, barren landscape under a likely sulfur-yellow sky.

Volcanic and tectonic activity appear to have wiped out most of Venus' ancient surface, with much of what we see today possibly no older than 150 million years. The planet is dotted with thousands of volcanoes and features two continent-sized highland regions: Ishtar Terra, roughly the size of Australia, and Aphrodite Terra, roughly the size of South America — plus a mountain that, at 11 km, edges out Mount Everest. Oddly, aside from Earth, Venus has fewer impact craters than any other rocky planet.
