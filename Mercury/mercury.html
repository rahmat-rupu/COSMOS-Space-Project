Overview

Mercury runs hot and cold in the most extreme way of any planet: daytime highs near the Sun can hit 430°C, but with no atmosphere to hold onto that heat, nights plunge to around -180°C. Despite sitting closest to the Sun, Mercury isn't actually the hottest planet — Venus takes that title thanks to its thick, heat-trapping atmosphere. What Mercury does hold the record for is speed, completing a full orbit around the Sun in just 88 Earth days.

Namesake

Mercury is named for the swiftest of the Roman gods — a fitting choice for the fastest-moving planet in the solar system.

Potential for life

Between the extreme temperature swings and heavy solar radiation, Mercury's environment is about as inhospitable to life as they come.

Mercury's orbit is a stretched-out ellipse, swinging from about 47 million km to 70 million km from the Sun, and it travels through space at nearly 47 km per second — faster than any other planet. It also rotates slowly, taking 59 Earth days to spin once. Near its closest approach to the Sun, the two effects combine strangely: from some spots on the surface, the Sun appears to rise, dip back down, and rise again before finally setting.

Moons

Mercury has no moons.

Rings

Mercury has no rings.

Formation

Mercury formed about 4.5 billion years ago, as gravity gathered gas and dust into the innermost planet of the solar system. Like the other rocky planets, it has a central core, a rocky mantle, and a solid crust.

Structure

Mercury is the second-densest planet after Earth, thanks to an oversized metallic core that takes up about 85% of its radius — roughly 2,074 km — with evidence suggesting part of it is still molten. Everything above that core, the mantle and crust combined, forms a comparatively thin shell only about 400 km thick.

Surface

Mercury looks a lot like Earth's Moon — heavily pockmarked with impact craters from billions of years of collisions. Its features are traditionally named after deceased artists, musicians, and writers, including figures like Dr. Seuss and Alvin Ailey. Two of its largest impact basins, Caloris (about 1,550 km across) and Rachmaninoff (about 306 km across), formed from asteroid strikes early in the solar system's history.

Beyond craters, Mercury has smooth plains as well as towering cliffs — some running hundreds of km long and up to a mile high — pushed up as the planet's interior cooled and shrank over billions of years. Most of the surface would look greyish-brown up close, but fresh impacts leave bright "crater rays," streaks of finely crushed, highly reflective rock flung outward from the impact site; these rays gradually darken over time as space weathering wears them down. Despite the blistering daytime heat, permanently shadowed craters near Mercury's poles may hide pockets of water ice.

Atmosphere

Mercury doesn't have a true atmosphere — just a thin exosphere made of atoms knocked loose from the surface by the solar wind and meteoroid impacts, made up mostly of oxygen, sodium, hydrogen, helium, and potassium.

Magnetosphere

Mercury's magnetic field is offset from the planet's equator and, at the surface, only about 1% as strong as Earth's. Even so, it interacts with the solar wind's own magnetic field in a way that can create intense "magnetic tornadoes," funneling hot solar wind plasma straight down to the surface and knocking loose particles that arc high above the planet.
